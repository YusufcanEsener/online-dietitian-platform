Estimation of daily energy 
requirements using a hybrid 
artificial intelligence model
Zeliha Uçar
1 & Betül Çiçek
2
Accurately estimating energy requirements is critical for individuals to maintain a healthy life. 
Traditional methods may be time-consuming, complex, low in accuracy, and costly, thus creating a 
need for new approaches. This study explores the applicability of hybrid artificial intelligence models 
for calculating daily energy requirements based on individuals’ anthropometric measurements and 
demographic data. The study’s primary goal is to develop a model that offers a reliable and practical 
solution with higher accuracy than existing methods while ensuring ease of use in field settings. This 
study used data collected from volunteer individuals at Sakarya University between September 2023 
and February 2024. Anthropometric measurements were performed by a bioelectrical impedance 
analysis (BIA) device, and demographic data were obtained through face-to-face surveys. Eighty-seven 
features were analyzed using the Spearman feature selection algorithm, and these were utilized to 
estimate energy requirements. Based on collaborative hybridization, the hybrid artificial intelligence 
model integrates three distinct Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) models using squared exponential, 
rational quadratic, and Matern52 kernels. These models were structured based on gender, and 
performance evaluation was carried out using criteria such as MAPE, MAD, MSE, R, and R². The best 
model performance in males was achieved at level 10 with 100% R², while the highest accuracy in 
females was observed at level 15. To increase model simplicity, the PCA method was applied; however, 
a decrease in performance was detected (R = 0.48, R² = 0.23). The accuracy of the artificial intelligence 
models proposed in this study was significantly higher than that of traditional formulas commonly 
preferred in the current literature. Hybrid artificial intelligence models offer practicality, accuracy, 
and flexibility in estimating energy requirements. Gender-based modeling has enhanced prediction 
performance while providing more reliable results by accounting for individual differences. This 
approach holds significant potential for advancing health and nutritional sciences.

nutritional monitoring, AI-Assisted dietary analysis
Energy is the capacity to perform work1,2. For both humans and animals, energy expenditure is one of the 
fundamental characteristics3. The energy required is obtained from nutrients, which are classified as 
carbohydrates, proteins, and fats. Humans receive these nutrients from plant- and animal-based foods in their 
diets1,2. Factors such as gender, age, level of physical activity, body weight, and height determine the energy 
necessary for sustaining life. The energy needed to support optimal growth and development, as well as to 
maintain life based on these factors, is referred to as “energy requirement”2,4.
Individuals can maintain a healthy life by balancing energy requirements and energy expenditure. Thus, 
energy expenditure plays a crucial role in calculating energy requirements. Total energy expenditure over 24 h 
consists of three main components: basal metabolic rate, activity-induced thermogenesis, and the thermic effect 
of foods. Basal metabolic rate is the minimum energy expended to sustain life, accounting for approximately 
65% of the total daily energy expenditure in sedentary individuals2,5. It is measured while the individual rests in 
a thermoneutral environment following an overnight fast3. Activity-induced thermogenesis, also known as the 
thermic effect of physical activity, refers to the energy required for daily tasks and bodily movement, constituting 
about 25% of the total daily energy expenditure in sedentary individuals. Lastly, the thermic effect of foods—
also referred to as diet-induced thermogenesis—covers the energy required for food consumption, digestion,

Numerous equations are used to estimate energy requirements6. In addition to various influencing factors, 
these equations consider the energy required for new tissue synthesis during growth and development periods, 
lactation, illness, and injury, as well as the individual’s total energy expenditure over 24 h7. Furthermore, 
body weight reflects the balance between energy intake and expenditure. Any imbalance in energy affects 
body weight2,4. To maintain this balance, it is crucial to determine energy requirements accurately1,8. This is 
especially important in the context of malnutrition, obesity, and diabetes, which have become global public 
health concerns9.
The unit used when measuring energy is the calorie. However, since the energy released during nutrient 
metabolism is significant, kilocalorie (kcal) is used as the standard unit. There are many different methods, 
both direct and indirect, for estimating energy requirements. These methods include direct calorimetry, indirect 
calorimetry, respiratory quotient, and the double-labelled water method2,10. Indirect calorimetry is accepted 
as the gold standard2,11. This method involves measuring the individual’s oxygen consumption and carbon 
dioxide production over a specific period. The measurement uses a mask or a ventilated hood that covers the 
mouth and nose and collects the exhaled carbon dioxide. Various software tools are available to analyze these 
measurements, though they are often expensive. Their accuracy and reliability are very high2,11,12. This method 
is suitable for both field and laboratory applications. Indirect calorimetry is the most commonly used method to 
assess energy efficiency by measuring oxygen consumption. Calculations are made using an energy equivalent 
of 5 kcal per liter of oxygen consumed by the body. It can measure oxygen consumption and calculate energy 
expenditure under different conditions, such as resting, running, or sleeping9. Moreover, it can be conducted to 
examine the energy requirements of individuals under different metabolic conditions or to evaluate the effects of 
various dietary models13. However, specific protocols should be followed for these measurements to be accurate. 
These include avoiding caffeine for at least 4 h before measurement and refraining from smoking and alcohol 
consumption for at least 2 h beforehand12.
The first calorimeter used for measuring energy requirements was the direct calorimeter9. These are costly 
devices that require specialized equipment. Their operation necessitates the presence of full-time technicians. 
Compared to indirect calorimeters, direct calorimeters provide less data. They measure the heat lost within a 
chamber10. Due to the requirement of conducting the measurement in an enclosed space, their accuracy in 
energy measurement is lower than that of indirect calorimeters9.
In the double-labelled water method, individuals are given an oral dose, followed by the measurement of 
the labelled water to estimate energy requirements. This method is suitable for use in vulnerable groups such 
as premature infants, newborns, adolescents, pregnant and lactating women. It is a non-invasive method ideal 
for community use, with high accuracy9,14. While the double-labelled water method can calculate total energy 
requirements, it does not provide detailed information regarding physical activity type, intensity, or duration. 
In this method, laboratory equipment is required to analyze biological samples, and the sample analysis process 
is complex and costly (R = 0.85)9. In a study conducted by Wong and colleagues in 2014, the double labelled 
water method is repeatable for measuring energy expenditure, can be used to estimate daily energy intake, and 
is suitable for monitoring changes in body composition over a 2.5–4.4-year period14. In addition to all these 
methods, various other approaches have recently been developed, such as accelerometers, heart rate monitors, 
sensewear armbands, and smart devices for monitoring energy expenditure and physical activity. These are also 
referred to as wearable devices. They are lightweight, portable technological tools. Being non-invasive, these 
devices have recently become increasingly popular. Although their accuracy is assumed to rely on heart rate 
monitoring and is considered lower than the devices above, their affordability and suitability for community 
applications refer to them as a preferred option15.
Due to the high cost, complexity, unsuitability for community use, and the burden placed on participants by 
precise measurement devices under free-living conditions, accelerometers have been developed as an alternative 
method. Accelerometers are produced in various types, such as single- or tri-axial models, and may be worn 
on the hip or waist. However, these measurements may vary due to factors like age (R²=0.04–0.08)16. In a study 
conducted in 2022 on British Army officers, energy expenditure of the individuals was measured by a wrist-worn 
accelerometer (R = 0.8)17.
There are many different formulas used to estimate energy expenditure6. However, their reliability is lower 
than the measurement methods mentioned above. Nevertheless, their use is more common in the field due to 
their affordability and practicality. These formulas first calculate basal metabolic rate, adding a physical activity 
factor. In this way, total energy expenditure is obtained. Standard formulas include Harris-Benedict, Mifflin-
St. Jeor and World Health Organization (WHO) equations, among others5,18,19. Harris and Benedict published 
the first of these, including parameters such as age, body weight, height, and gender5,19. Their study involved 
239 adults aged between 16 and 63, including 136 men and 103 women (ER = 0.8, KR = 0.6)19. New cross-
validated equations were developed after researchers identified deviations in this formula3. Owen and colleagues 
published two studies evaluating the requirements of men and women aged 18–65. The studies involved 60 men 
and 44 women. Resting metabolic rate and the thermic effect of food were determined by indirect calorimetry, 
and separate regression equations were developed for men and women20,21. In 1990, Mifflin and colleagues 
developed a new formula through multiple regression analysis with a sample of 498 healthy individuals (247 
women, 251 men) using indirect calorimetry (R = 0.71)18. Although many equations exist, new ones continue to 
be developed3. One such example is the formula developed by Gerrior and colleagues in 2006, which includes 
age, height, and body weight based on gender. A formula was created by combining basal metabolic rate with 
the metabolic equivalent to calculate the physical activity factor. Then, grouping was made according to this 
formula to estimate the effect of physical activity on energy expenditure, and energy requirement was calculated 
by multiplying the two values7. In a 2022 study, the accuracy of equations used to estimate energy expenditure

Another method used to estimate energy requirements is the calculation based on energy intake. This 
approach relies on the individual’s dietary intake records. However, since it is based on recall and self-reporting, 
it often leads to inaccuracies in estimation2. In a study utilizing dietary intake records, Thai workers aged between 
20 and 50 years were included. Using the 24-hour recall method, nutritional data were collected over two days. 
The average energy intake was 1485 kcal/day for men and 1428 kcal/day for women, respectively28. In another 
similar study conducted in China, the energy intake of children aged 6-to-17 years was calculated using the 24-
hour recall method. A three-day record was taken, and the average intake was calculated29. As mentioned above, 
although numerous methods are available in the literature, there remains a need for reliable, AI-based systems 
that are practical, highly accurate, and suitable for use in field settings with the integration of newer technologies.
Recent studies have increasingly applied machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) methods 
to estimate human energy requirements and metabolic rate. For example, probabilistic graphical models such 
as bootstrap particle filters have been used to combine continuous heart rate and core body temperature data, 
significantly improving the accuracy of metabolic rate prediction (RMSE = 133 ± 26 W)30. Smartphone sensors, 
including heart rate monitors and inertial sensors, have also been evaluated for estimating energy expenditure 
during daily activities and sports, showing performance comparable to or better than commercial devices such as 
the SenseWear armband31. Neural network-based approaches have likewise been explored; for instance, models 
developed with General Regression Neural Networks (GRNN) and Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLP) using body 
composition data outperformed traditional equations like Harris-Benedict and Mifflin-St Jeor, achieving higher 
correlation (R = 0.85) and lower error (RMSE = 134.91 kcal/day)32. Other studies have integrated AI with sensor 
systems such as Kinect cameras to estimate metabolic rate across different physical activities, offering simplified 
alternatives to conventional measurement techniques33. Furthermore, regression-based models have been 
embedded into web applications, enabling the prediction of total daily energy expenditure from anthropometric 
and body composition variables without relying on physical activity level inputs34. Beyond direct estimation 
of energy expenditure, wearable devices employing ML algorithms have also been investigated for related 
applications such as non-invasive blood glucose forecasting in diabetes management35. These studies highlight 
the growing role of ML and AI in improving the accuracy, accessibility, and personalization of metabolic rate 
and energy requirement estimation.
Recent studies have increasingly employed machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) methods 
to estimate human energy requirements and metabolic rate. For instance, particle filter-based probabilistic 
graphical models have been applied using heart rate and core body temperature data, achieving high accuracy 
in metabolic rate estimation30. Similarly, Kinect camera–based AI approaches have enabled metabolic rate 
estimation using simple postural and activity observations33. Other studies have applied machine learning 
models such as Random Forest, trained on motion sensor data, to predict activity-specific metabolic rate with 
accuracies exceeding 90%36. In addition, cloud-based boosted decision tree regression has been explored for 
energy expenditure prediction from physiological sensor data, highlighting the scalability of AI applications 
in health monitoring37. More recently, neural network models such as General Regression Neural Networks 
(GRNN) have been developed to predict resting metabolic rate, demonstrating superior accuracy over traditional 
predictive equations (R = 0.85)32. In addition to model development, effective feature selection is crucial in 
improving prediction accuracy and computational efficiency in AI-based health applications. Recent work, such 
as the Binary Arithmetic Optimization Algorithm (BAOA) with a K-Nearest Neighbor classifier, has shown 
strong potential to identify the most relevant variables and reduce dimensionality, which could also be valuable 
when modeling energy requirements38. Collectively, these studies illustrate the growing role of AI and ML in 
developing accurate, scalable, and accessible tools for energy requirement estimation.
However, unlike traditional equations that rely on fixed parameters and linear assumptions, AI-based 
approaches can capture complex, non-linear interactions between demographic and anthropometric variables, 
making them more adaptable and practically valuable for diverse real-world settings.
Although numerous methods exist for estimating energy requirements, many remain limited in accuracy, 
practicality, or adaptability for field applications. Therefore, the development of artificial intelligence–based

This study uses artificial intelligence based on anthropometric measurements and demographic data to 
calculate individuals’ daily energy requirements. The proposed method provides reliable, field-applicable, 
technician-free, fast, and highly accurate estimations. Therefore, demographic information and results from 
bioelectrical impedance measurements—which are easy to obtain—were utilized. A feature selection algorithm 
was applied during the analysis phase to enhance model performance. Additionally, gender-specific models were 
developed to improve accuracy further. Since body composition differs between men and women, gender-based 
models have the advantage of yielding better performance39. Performance evaluation criteria for the developed 
models were calculated and compared with existing studies in the literature.
Materials and methods
The study was conducted in accordance with the flowchart presented in Fig. 1. In the first step, data acquisition 
included anthropometric measurements, demographic information, and outputs obtained from bioelectrical 
impedance analysis (BIA). Then, data preprocessing was performed, which involved handling missing values, 
normalization, and removal of outliers. The preprocessed data were directed into two dimensionality reduction/
feature selection pathways: Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Spearman correlation-based feature 
selection. The resulting feature subsets were subsequently used as inputs for three different Gaussian Process 
Regression (GPR) models: GPR-S (Squared Exponential kernel), GPR-R (Rational Quadratic kernel), GPR-M 
(Matern52 kernel). Finally, the outputs of these models were combined under a hybrid machine learning 
algorithm using an ensemble averaging strategy to obtain the final prediction. Since males and females differ 
significantly in terms of body composition40, gender-specific models were also developed in this study to 
improve predictive accuracy and account for physiological variation.

Measurements
Data were collected from individuals through questionnaire forms by face-to-face interview method, and

The questionnaires included data on participants’ demographic characteristics and physical activity status. 
Parameters obtained via BIA, such as body weight (kg), lean mass weight (kg), lean mass percentage (%), fat mass 
(kg), fat percentage (%), body fluid weight (kg), and fluid percentage (%), were used in the analyses (Table 2).
Bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) was conducted using the Tanita BC-601 Segmental Body Composition 
Monitor (Tanita Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). This device employs an 8-electrode segmental measurement system 
providing whole-body and regional assessments (arms, legs, trunk). It has a maximum weight capacity of 150 kg, 
with a measurement precision of 100 g for body weight and 0.1% for body fat percentage. The device operates at a 
frequency of 50 kHz, 500 µA, a low-level current that complies with international safety standards for biological 
measurements. The BC-601 also allows storage and transfer of results via SD card and proprietary software. 
Calibration was performed automatically per the manufacturer’s guidelines before each measurement session. 
The validity and reliability of this model have been confirmed in previous clinical studies, and it is widely used 
in research settings for body composition analysis.
Energy requirement calculation
Energy requirements were calculated based on age, height, and gender using the formula established by the 
World Health Organization (WHO) (Eqs. 1 and 2). In this formula, the constant value is accepted as 1 for males 
(Eqs. 1) and 0.95 for females (Eq. 2)41. The authors do not select these constants, but are predefined in the 
WHO guidelines to account for metabolic differences between males and females. Physical activity level (PAL) 
is a fixed coefficient that ranges between 1 and 2.5 depending on the daily activity level of the individual, and 
is incorporated into the Eqs7,41. PAL values of 1–1.39 are used for sedentary activity levels, 1.4–1.59 for light 
activity, 1.6–1.89 for moderate activity, and 1.9–2.5 for high physical activity levels, respectively41.

Machine learning
This study’s most successful machine learning models included Gaussian process regression (GPR) with squared-
exponential, rational-quadratic, and Matern52 kernels and a hybrid algorithm.
GPR model
Gaussian process regression (GPR) models are flexible, robust, and non-parametric. They are preferred in high-
dimensional, small-sample, or non-linear datasets42. GPR works by learning non-linear mappings through 
kernel functions, constructing a covariance matrix between input features and real-valued outputs. In this way, 
it provides Gaussian uncertainty estimates for predictions, which enables integration into predictive control, 
adaptive control, and Bayesian filtering techniques43.
GPR is simpler compared to neural networks and support vector machines. It possesses non-parametric 
flexibility and may self-adjust its hyperparameters43. However, some recent approaches—such as the variance-
adjusted gradient boosting algorithm—have enhanced the performance of GPR, making it comparable to or 
even superior to other regression methods like random forests and gradient boosting machines42.
GPR models are commonly used to fit data when the regression function is unknown44. For instance, they are 
widely utilized in system modeling, forecasting, control techniques, and industrial processes43,45,46. Additionally, 
GPR has been applied in diverse areas as estimating unknown functions, modeling and forecasting human 
mortality rates, and predicting the safety margin of a nuclear power plant46.
GPR models may not be effectively used with large datasets due to their high computational complexity. 
They scale cubically with the number of observations, restricting their use to small- and medium-sized datasets. 
Consequently, GPR models are powerful tools for probabilistic regression however, their applicability to large-
scale data remains limited47.

GPR squared exponential (GPRS), GPR rational quadratic (GPRR), and GPR matern52 (GPRM) are different 
kernel functions used within the GPR framework. Each function has distinct characteristics that influence the 
behavior and performance of the GPR model.
To enhance model performance, hyperparameters of the Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) models (e.g., 
kernel length-scale and variance) were optimized using automated search strategies. These strategies included 
Bayesian optimization and randomized/grid search techniques, which iteratively adjust parameter values to 
minimize prediction error based on log-marginal likelihood. This process ensured that the selected kernel 
configurations provided the best trade-off between model accuracy and generalizability.
GPR squared exponential
GPR squared exponential (GPRS), also known as the Radial Basis Function (RBF), operates assuming the 
modeled function is smooth and continuous. Its flexibility and smoothness contribute to its frequent use. It is an 
effective method for capturing complex data relationships48.
GPR rational quadratic
It is a mixture of GPRS functions with different length scales. Compared to GPRS, it is more flexible and capable 
of producing models with varying degrees of smoothness3. This kernel is particularly used when the data exhibit 
different levels of variability45.
GPR matern52
It is smoother than the GPRS kernel. However, it behaves more flexibly when constructing rougher models.

Hybrid machine learning algorithm
It is a combination of different algorithms aimed at solving complex problems. By integrating the advantages 
of multiple algorithms, it seeks to create a more powerful solution method49,50. There are two distinct types of 
hybridization: collaborative and integrative. Collaborative hybridization approaches the problem by applying the 
algorithms sequentially or in parallel, allowing for information exchange. Integrative hybridization, on the other 
hand, focuses on different aspects of the problem-solving process for each algorithm51.
Hybrid algorithms enable more efficient and effective outcomes by combining various techniques. 
They improve exploration strategies and enhance solution quality52. They exhibit better performance than 
conventional algorithms, particularly in solving non-deterministic polynomial-time challenging optimization 
problems, thereby generating a synergistic effect53. These algorithms have been successfully applied to real-
world problems52,53. In summary, hybrid algorithms combine the strengths of different approaches to offer more 
balanced and practical solutions, leading to successful implementations.

with different kernels (squared exponential, rational quadratic, and Matern52). Each model was trained 
independently, and their prediction outputs were combined by taking the simple arithmetic mean (unweighted

Performance evaluation criterion
In machine learning models, performance evaluation criteria (PEC) are essential for selecting the appropriate 
model for a given task. Depending on the characteristics of the model and user expectations, various experiments 
may be designed to assess performance. Techniques such as cross-validation and bootstrapping may be used 
for performance evaluation in supervised and unsupervised models54. In addition, metrics like accuracy, 
precision, recall, F1 score, and area under the ROC curve are key indicators for assessing model performance55. 
In supervised learning, models are trained with input data and target variables. In unsupervised learning, only 
input data is used, and the algorithm attempts to identify patterns within the data56. In the current study, to 
evaluate the results, the following metrics were employed: Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE), Mean 
Absolute Deviation (MAD), Standard Error (SE), Mean Squared Error (MSE), Root Mean Squared Error 
(RMSE), R, and R² (Eqs. 1–5).
MAPE provides a mapping procedure based on measurable criteria and allows for a visual presentation of 
the results57. It is used in research and development organizations to evaluate performance against specified 
targets58,59. MAPE is calculated by averaging the absolute percentage errors between predicted and actual values. 
It is a simple and easily interpretable method; however, it is sensitive to outliers and unsuitable for cases where 
exact values are zero or near zero60.
MAD is a method that estimates dispersion for continuous and finite-valued random variables without 
relying on absolute values61. It is especially appropriate when data are skewed and is easy to apply and interpret62.
MSE measures the average of the squared errors or deviations and provides detailed insights into model 
performance63–65. SE measures the accuracy of predictions and indicates the precision of the estimates66.
R² shows the proportion of variance in the dependent variable that the independent variables can explain. 
However, when used alone as a PEC, it may be insufficient, as it does not indicate which aspects of model 
performance are strong or weak67–69. Among the PECs used in this study, values of MAPE, MAD, SE, MSE, and 
RMSE are expected to be close to 0, while R and R² are expected to be close to 1.
In addition to the classical error-based metrics (MAPE, MAD, SE, MSE, RMSE, R, and R²), computational 
efficiency indicators were also considered: Prediction Speed (PS) expressed as observations predicted per 
second, Training Time (TT) measured in seconds required for model training, and Model Size (MS) measured in 
kilobytes. These parameters provide practical insights into the computational cost and feasibility of the models, 
which are critical for clinical and field applications.

k-fold cross-validation
To ensure the robustness and generalizability of the developed models, k-fold cross-validation was employed. 
This approach randomly partitioned the dataset into k equally sized folds. At each iteration, one fold was used 
as the validation set, while the remaining k-1 folds were used for training. This process was repeated k times so 
that each fold was used once for validation. The final performance metrics were obtained by averaging the results 
across all folds. This procedure reduces the risk of overfitting and provides a more reliable estimate of model 
performance, particularly when working with relatively small datasets. This study set k to 10, a commonly used 
choice in the literature for balancing computational efficiency and statistical reliability.
Principal component analysis (PCA)
Strong correlations among high-dimensional datasets may increase model complexity and reduce predictive 
accuracy. For this reason, dimensionality reduction methods are frequently employed in AI-based modeling 
studies to maintain model simplicity while enhancing generalizability. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) 
is a statistical technique that represents correlated variables with fewer independent components, thereby 
eliminating redundancies in the data while preserving information content70,71.
This study applied PCA directly to the dataset and subsequently integrated into the AI-based modeling 
process. PCA was used independently as an alternative dimensionality reduction method, rather than in 
conjunction with Spearman-based feature selection.
Computational platform
All computational analyses were conducted using MATLAB R2024b (MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA, USA). 
Standard toolboxes such as the Statistics and Machine Learning Toolbox were utilized where available, while 
all remaining procedures—including feature selection implementation, hybrid model construction, and 
performance evaluation—were coded manually by the authors. This ensured flexibility in model development 
and allowed adaptation of the algorithms to the specific requirements of the study.
Results
This study aimed to estimate individuals’ energy requirements using artificial intelligence-based models 
developed with demographic information and bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) measurements. Eighty-

individuals was utilized to train artificial intelligence models. Separate models were also developed to enhance 
model performance for male and female participants. Furthermore, feature selection algorithms and Principal 
Component Analysis (PCA) were applied to improve model performance.

A perfect performance (R² = 1.00) was observed in several of the developed models. This outcome should be 
interpreted cautiously and can be attributed to several factors. First, the relatively homogeneous dataset, derived 
from a specific and limited population group, may have facilitated the models in capturing strong relationships 
between input features and energy requirements. Second, the selected features at the optimal levels (10 for males, 
15 for females, and 11 for the combined group) were highly correlated with basal metabolic rate, enabling the 
models to identify nearly perfect associations. Finally, owing to its non-parametric and kernel-based structure, 
the Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) algorithm is highly flexible in learning linear and non-linear interactions. 
This may explain its ability to achieve an exact fit under such conditions. While these results indicate strong 
internal consistency, further external studies with larger and more heterogeneous datasets are recommended.
In this study, 10-fold cross-validation (k = 10) was also applied to strengthen the internal validity of the models. 
The cross-validation results demonstrated that the models consistently achieved outstanding performance at 
feature levels 10 for males, 15 for females, and 11 for the combined dataset (R = 1, R² = 1). In addition, the 
computational costs were evaluated, revealing that prediction speeds reached thousands of observations per 
second, training times remained within seconds to a few minutes, and model sizes were relatively small. These 
findings indicate that the proposed models are highly accurate, computationally efficient, and feasible for 
practical clinical and field applications.
In addition to predictive accuracy, the analysis of computational costs (Tables 6 and 7) provides further 
insights into the practicality of the proposed models. Specifically, prediction speeds reached hundreds of 
thousands of observations per second, training times remained within fractions of a second to a few minutes 
depending on model type, and model sizes were manageable, typically ranging from a few hundred kilobytes 
to a few megabytes. These characteristics confirm that the developed AI-based models are statistically robust, 
computationally efficient, and scalable for real-world applications.
In addition, a critical limitation of this study is the lack of an external validation dataset. Although 10-fold 
cross-validation was performed to enhance the robustness of the results and reduce the risk of overfitting, the 
absence of independent testing restricts the generalizability of the models. Therefore, future studies should 
incorporate external and more heterogeneous cohorts to confirm and extend the applicability of the proposed 
models. Furthermore, the computational efficiency of the proposed models is an essential consideration for 
real-world applications. In this study, the GPR-based models demonstrated sufficient efficiency to produce 
predictions within seconds for small- to medium-sized datasets, supporting their applicability in clinical and 
field settings. However, due to the cubic computational complexity of GPR, scalability challenges may arise when 
working with much larger and more heterogeneous datasets. Future work should therefore explore optimized or 
approximation-based approaches—such as sparse GPR to reduce kernel matrix complexity, lightweight neural 
approximations to retain non-linear mapping at lower cost, or model compression/distillation techniques to 
balance accuracy and efficiency—to reduce computational costs in large-scale applications.

integrating such models into mobile health applications and clinical decision support systems.
In this study, applying Principal Component Analysis (PCA) resulted in a noticeable decline in prediction 
performance (R = 0.48, R² = 0.23). The main reason for this reduction is that PCA is a linear transformation 
method that projects the original features into orthogonal components. While this approach successfully reduces 
dimensionality and multicollinearity, it may simultaneously eliminate or weaken non-linear interactions among 
the features, which are crucial for accurate energy requirement prediction. In particular, the initial principal

accelerometers to estimate energy expenditure. Moreover, the study highlights the importance of considering 
accelerometer placement and participant demographic characteristics16. Artificial intelligence-based models 
have been demonstrated to possess a higher accuracy level than the approach presented by Dougherty et al.
Indirect calorimetry is the gold standard for estimating energy requirements2,11. However, measurement 
with indirect calorimetry is labor-intensive and requires trained technical staff. The calculation is performed 
by measuring the carbon dioxide exhaled and the oxygen consumed using a mask that covers the mouth and 
nose. This process requires the use of specialized, high-cost software2,11,12. However, specific protocols should be 
followed to conduct these measurements accurately. For instance, individuals should refrain from consuming 
caffeine for at least 4 h and avoid drinking alcohol and smoking for at least 2 h before the measurement12. 
The model proposed in this study may estimate energy requirements using simpler parameters than the gold 
standard. Therefore, the proposed model represents a more easily applicable method. In addition, artificial 
intelligence software enables energy requirement estimations to be performed at a lower cost. Moreover, the 
models proposed in this study do not require any food/beverage consumption-related restrictions. This feature 
allows for the convenience of performing measurements at any time of the day.
In addition to the gold standard of indirect calorimetry commonly used in the literature, several other 
methods, such as the double-labeled water technique and direct calorimetry, are also available2,10 Although the 
results obtained from all these methods have a high degree of accuracy, they also have certain disadvantages. 
For instance, direct calorimetry measures the heat lost within a controlled room environment10Therefore, this 
method is unsuitable for use in field settings. In contrast, the model used in this study may estimate energy 
requirements without requiring a specialized environment. On the other hand, the double-labeled water method 
estimates energy requirements through oral administration of isotopes. It is a complex and costly method9. 
Unlike the double-labeled water method, this study presents a more straightforward and cost-effective artificial 
intelligence model. In addition, there is no need for technical staff. Considering the features of the proposed 
model, it may also be suitable for clinical use. In the literature, numerous studies estimate energy requirements 
using the abovementioned methods. In the current study, artificial intelligence-based models have higher R

models incorporating body composition and resting metabolic rate reached values as high as 0.7425. When R² 
values are compared, the artificial intelligence-based model may provide more accurate predictions, while the 
method developed using the double-labeled water technique may be more practical and applicable.

Conclusion
There are several factors underlying the success of the model we developed. First, the anthropometric 
measurements and demographic data used in the model are key factors influencing energy requirements. The 
accurate and comprehensive inclusion of these variables in the model has enhanced its performance. Secondly, 
the artificial intelligence algorithms used in the model effectively analyze the data and accurately estimate energy 
requirements. Finally, during the model’s development process, its performance was tested and optimized using 
different datasets. Thus, the model may be successfully applied across various populations and conditions.
Estimating energy requirements is used in many fields, particularly in dietary planning and counseling, 
healthcare, and for athletes. In this study, energy requirement estimation was performed using artificial 
intelligence based on BIA measurements and demographic data. The proposed models have several advantages 
over existing devices and models in the literature. These include: (1) energy requirements may be calculated by 
BIA measurements and demographic data, which are easy to obtain; (2) a highly accurate artificial intelligence-
based system has been developed; (3) it is easy to use and does not require technical expertise/staff; (4) it does not 
require additional installation costs; and (5) the system saves time. Considering all these features, the proposed 
model is evaluated as more advanced, practical than the literature, and suitable for daily use.
In future research, it will be essential to validate the proposed models on larger and more diverse datasets, 
including individuals from different ethnic backgrounds, age groups, and clinical conditions. Such external 
validation will help assess the generalizability of the models and ensure their applicability in broader real-world 
healthcare and nutritional contexts. Moreover, integrating additional physiological or lifestyle-related variables 
could further enhance predictive accuracy and practical utility.
An additional strength of the present study is demonstrating that the proposed models achieve superior 
predictive accuracy compared to traditional equations and high computational efficiency. The models showed 
very short training times, rapid prediction speeds, and compact model sizes, indicating their suitability for 
integration into mobile health applications, clinical decision support systems, and large-scale population studies.
